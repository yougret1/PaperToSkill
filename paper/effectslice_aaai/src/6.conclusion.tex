\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Provenance and sufficiency answer different questions.}
A source locator establishes where an instruction came from, and dependency closure establishes that retained procedural units include their declared prerequisites. Neither property shows that the resulting specification is sufficient for an agent. Conversely, a high private score without a source map cannot explain which paper content the artifact represents. SkillAudit joins these views without conflating them: the atom graph explains the candidate boundary, while matched $B/F/S$ execution tests the local substitution claim.

\paragraph{The full specification is an active control, not an oracle.}
The SNAP result shows why $F$ must be evaluated rather than assumed correct. When $F$ is insufficient, a poor $S$ score cannot be cleanly attributed to simplification. When $F$ succeeds and $S$ fails, as for Toolformer prefix 01, the candidate-specific diagnosis is stronger. When both succeed and $B$ does not, as for prefix 04, acceptance is warranted only under the frozen task, model, tests, and thresholds. This three-way comparison is more informative than reporting candidate accuracy alone.

\paragraph{Invalidity is a scientific result.}
The cross-paper stress test does not provide the desired effectiveness evidence, but it exposes two concrete specification defects: ambiguity about the input wrapper and ambiguity about exact output fields. The forward clarification removes the first failure class without changing historical evidence and then reveals the second. Treating all of these rows as ordinary score zero would conceal the measurement problem. An auditable validator should preserve this distinction even when it lowers the apparent headline result.

\paragraph{Acceptance is deliberately local.}
An accepted candidate is not ``the core'' of a paper in the abstract. It is a candidate that substituted for $F$ under $(t,\Omega)$. A reusable release should therefore package $S$ with its paper identity, atom boundary, task, model, scorer, private-registry identity, schedule, and field-level rationale. If any of those factors change, the previous record remains useful provenance but does not automatically authorize reuse.

\section{Limitations and Broader Impact}

The positive behavioral evidence is narrow. The focused suite covers two paper mechanisms, one primary provider model, one action scaffold, and finite 18-block schedules. Only one non-planted simplified candidate is accepted. Its selection used earlier evidence before a separate confirmatory boundary, so it is not a prospectively estimated discovery rate. The broad suite covers 12 papers and 24 tasks, but its 1,296 rows are dominated by interface failures. The five operationally successful rows are unbalanced across conditions and cannot support a paired effect. Full cross-paper efficacy therefore remains unestablished in this draft.

The atom boundaries, executable wording, dependencies, and completeness of $F$ are evaluation-package judgments. Digests prove identity, not semantic correctness. Independent paper experts should review these structures, and future work should measure inter-annotator agreement over atomization and dependency edges. Atom restoration/removal experiments would also provide stronger causal evidence that a particular omitted source unit caused a candidate failure.

The acceptance thresholds are prespecified engineering parameters, not universal constants or calibrated population guarantees. The focused count rule tolerates fresh-session variability, but it does not establish trajectory equivalence. Provider-side sampling is not fully controllable: deterministic case seeds, ordering, and temperature settings do not imply reproducible backend routing or sampling when the API exposes no enforceable random seed. Repetition across independently operated providers and additional hidden registries is needed before making broad robustness claims.

No human participants were studied. A user study is not required to test the internal validity of the finite substitution rule, but it is required for claims that SkillAudit helps researchers understand papers, reproduce methods faster, or avoid downstream scientific mistakes. Such a study should compare full specifications, unvalidated simplified candidates, and evidence-carrying accepted candidates on completion time, correction rate, and expert-rated fidelity.

SkillAudit may reduce overconfident reuse by making evidence boundaries visible. It may also create false assurance if an acceptance label is detached from its local task and scorer. Generated code and paper-derived tools retain normal supply-chain and execution risks; isolated workspaces, restricted tools, private scoring, and immutable evidence reduce but do not eliminate them. Releases should expose rejected and invalid cases as well as accepted ones to avoid selectively presenting only successful candidates.

\section{Conclusion}

We introduced SkillAudit, a traceable protocol for deciding whether a simplified paper-derived agent skill specification can replace its full counterpart under a frozen evaluation boundary. SkillAudit links specification units to exact source passages and dependencies, executes matched no-specification, full, and simplified conditions, validates evidence before scoring performance, and emits a local \textsc{Accept}, \textsc{Reject}, or \textsc{Invalid} record. In the focused suite, it distinguishes full-specification insufficiency from candidate-specific information loss, passes strict-subset and identity controls, and accepts one non-planted Toolformer candidate on separate held-out evidence. The 1,296-row cross-paper stress test further demonstrates the value of explicit invalidity diagnosis by localizing systematic input- and output-contract defects. These results support SkillAudit as an auditable validation layer, while leaving broad cross-paper effectiveness to a corrected, fully paired evaluation.
